Flight from the Dark

Joe Dever's debut is so good it made an arbitrary death forgivable.

Flight from the Dark was first published in 1984, written by Joe Dever, and is the first book in the Lone Wolf series.

Although I'd never read any Lone Wolf books as a kid, I had heard great things about the series, so I happily picked up the box set online which gives you the first 12 books for about £3 per book. Even if the series turned out to be average, I felt that was a bargain.

Flight from the Dark is set on the fictional planet of Magnamund. You play Lone Wolf, a young Jedi Kai warrior whose Jedi Temple Kai Monastery is attacked by evil forces, led by the Darklords. Luckily, our intrepid hero was out gathering wood at the time of the attack and so manages to survive. As the hero, you must find a way to the capital city to inform the King of the attack. Luckily, you'll have your wits and your Jedi powers five Kai disciplines to aid you.

Alright, so I may have laid on the Star Wars parallels a little thick, but it's the first thing that stood out to me. I know many fantasy stories have magical powers, and it's not as if Star Wars was the first to do it. But "Jedis in Lord of the Rings with lots of forests" is, I think, a fair, albeit crass, summary of Flight from the Dark.

But I'm not attacking the book at all. Not even close. It was a mostly fantastic experience. Comparing any fantasy fiction to Lord of the Rings is such a banal observation at this point that it's gone far beyond a cliché, but it's obvious that there's a genuine love and affection for the story and the setting that feels Tolkien-esque. I felt like I was in the world. Sure, lots of names were thrown at me, but you know what? I cared enough to actually write them down. I did that because the author cared. Within thirty minutes of reading, I was fully invested.

Then there's the Kai disciplines. The book gives you a list of abilities your particular version of Lone Wolf can have, and each provides advantages at certain points during the story. This was a surprisingly effective mechanic. Every time I reached the end of a section and I had a Kai discipline for one of the available options, I genuinely felt a burst of excitement. Like a unique path was open to me or, perhaps more importantly, a danger would be avoided.

I should also praise the combat system. Without going into great detail, it tries to account for the differences in strength between your character and whatever monster you're fighting. If you're lucky, you might come out of a fight relatively unscathed, but there's a good chance that if you take on something equal to you, your character is going to get hurt. This makes fighting genuinely exciting. The most memorable moment during my playthroughs was a fight on my first run in which I barely made it out alive. Because the damage you deal and receive scales with your dice rolls, it feels fair. When fights dictated by dice rolls still manage to feel fair, I'm not sure a higher compliment can be paid.

The writing deserves its own mention. Never too flowery, but descriptive and immersive, with some sections running more than two pages in length. That might not sound like a lot for a novel, but for a gamebook it represents real depth. Choices are important, of course; they're the defining feature of interactive fiction, after all. But those choices feel so much richer, and carry so much more weight, if you understand the context, the setting, and the other characters. Dever does this wonderfully well.

Are there any negatives? Sadly, there is one I can't ignore. After the aforementioned epic fight on my first run, I died shortly thereafter due to an arbitrary death: that is, a death that had nothing to do with my choices, and for which I had no warning. I don't mind dying because of a fight, especially in a well-designed system like Lone Wolf's. I usually walk away smiling, because I was fairly defeated. The dice were unkind, but I knew what I was getting myself in for. Falling into a pit because I chose to go right instead of left, however? That's cheap, and it feels like contrived jeopardy. I won't be too hard on this book, as it really was enjoyable and I'm told it gets better in future instalments. But it is worth pointing out.

Despite this, I came back to the book twice and managed to finish it twice. Even so, I feel like I've only read perhaps half of its sections. There's clearly more to explore, and when I eventually finish this run of the Lone Wolf series I'll return to Flight from the Dark with different choices. That could be a while; this series is enormous. But even now, I'm looking forward to the day when I eventually return to this book.

Verdict

Flight from the Dark is a wonderfully written, engaging debut for the Lone Wolf series, and I fully understand why it became one of the best-selling gamebook series of all time. It's immersive, the disciplines mechanic adds real dimension to the gameplay, the combat feels fair, and you become genuinely invested in the world of Magnamund. If you can forgive the occasional arbitrary death, you can't go wrong with this book.
